About this portfolio

GoShuttle is a student transport initiative formalising a partnership between CPUT and Golden Arrow Bus Service (GABS) to run dedicated, subsidised bus routes from high-density residential areas — Khayelitsha, Mitchells Plain, Delft, Kraaifontein — to CPUT's Bellville and Cape Town campuses.

My role — Designer

I created all visual elements: the GoShuttle brand guide, logo, Figma wireframes, UI screens, charts, route maps, and presentation slides.

The team

Samukelisiwe Maphumulo (Writer & Team Leader), Mvuyisi Mcthwa (Researcher), Kwanda Mbayise (Presenter), Sinethemba Mkhoma (Designer).

How we worked

Kanban on Trello (Backlog → To Do → In Progress → Review → Done), MoSCoW prioritisation, 2-week sprints, weekly Friday reviews, and continuous user feedback from campus activation days.

01 · Evidence + Reflection

Agile Team Charter

A living, co-created social contract defining our shared purpose, values, working agreements and operational boundaries.

Evidence

1. Purpose & Vision

Mission: Help CPUT students who struggle with unreliable transport by providing access to dedicated buses, so they arrive on time and focus on their studies.

Success: A mapped pilot route covering ≥2 high-density pickup points with a proposed schedule, pricing model, and WhatsApp bot prototype ready for testing by end of year.

2. Roles

  • Sinethemba Mkhoma — Designer
  • Samukelisiwe Maphumulo — Writer & Leader
  • Mvuyisi Mcthwa — Researcher
  • Kwanda Mbayise — Presenter

3. Ways of Working

Kanban via Trello. Stand-up every Monday & Wednesday (15 min). Weekly team meeting every Friday. Sprint goals every two weeks.

4. Working Agreements

  • Show up prepared and on time
  • Respect timeboxes
  • Give constructive feedback
  • Raise blockers early
  • Embrace changing requirements

5. Communication

Channels: WhatsApp (quick/urgent), Email (formal), Microsoft Teams (meetings), Trello (daily progress).

Response: Urgent WhatsApp within 2 h (08:00–18:00); non-urgent within 24 h.

6. Decision-Making & Escalation

Consensus → Product Owner final decision → team vote. Deadlocks escalate to Friday meeting; if unresolved, researcher gathers data within 48 h and team revotes.

7. Definition of Done

  • Code completed & reviewed by the team
  • Research results recorded
  • Documents updated & agreed
  • Shared on Trello

8. Risks & Constraints

Risks: GABS may not commit without CPUT backing; survey responses below 200; WhatsApp bot needs skills beyond the team.

Constraints: No budget for incentives; Week 2–12 timeline; limited access to official CPUT student data.

Reflection

Writing the charter before we built anything turned out to be the single most useful hour of the project. As the designer, my work depended on the others — I couldn't produce a booking screen until the writer had confirmed the four home-area options, and I couldn't export final assets until the researcher had tested pricing messages with students. The charter's "Definition of Done" and the Trello column structure meant I could always see which upstream work was actually ready. The biggest growth for me was learning to treat blockers as a team conversation, not a personal failure — raising them early became normal after week two, and the design sprint moved much faster as a result. On reflection, the charter is the artefact I would show a future employer first: it proves we didn't just call ourselves Agile, we wrote down the rules that made it true.

Reflection

The most useful story-writing lesson came from a mistake: "Book a trip" started as one broad story, and it stalled on the Trello board for days because nobody could say when it was done. Splitting it into three tasks (select route & time → confirm fare → receive confirmation) meant I could design and test each task independently — and the "done" column finally started moving. As the designer I used the stories as a checklist for wireframes; if a story had no screen, I hadn't designed it yet. The trust story (US-05) is the one I'm most proud of, because it came directly from real student conversations on campus, not a hypothetical persona. That taught me that the best user stories are earned through contact with users, and the product gets visibly better when we let evidence, not opinions, choose the stories.

Reflection

MoSCoW was the tool that kept the backlog honest. Every idea — mine included — had to earn its place by proving a student would actually use it. My own designer instinct wanted dark mode and rich motion in sprint one; the backlog said "Won't Have" and the team was right, because the real bottleneck was trust and pricing, not polish. The biggest growth for me was learning to re-prioritise with evidence: after campus activation, the ~35% willingness to pay R20+ directly reshaped which pricing stories became Must. When students told us they mainly use phones, mobile support moved from a "Should" into the September sprint immediately. The backlog became a conversation about why things matter, not just a list of what to build — and that is the mindset I'll carry into every product team I join.

Sprint 2 (22–26 Sept) — Pass page + feedback + mobile

Tasks

  • Implement Pass page (view / top-up / purchase)
  • Feedback check-in after website review

Outcome

The Pass page went live. Feedback revealed students mostly use phones — we treated mobile support as a priority, not a "later" item.

Reflection

Sprint 1 taught me the difference between a plan and a working plan. The sprint goal — a clickable end-to-end flow — was deliberately narrow, and that focus let me finish Figma exports, then immediately switch into coded UI (Home + Booking + Tracking) and test it. When I hit the mismatch between Figma spacing and real mobile screen sizes on day one, the sprint backlog made the trade-off explicit: mobile-first wins, adjust the code, not the plan. The mid-sprint check-in mattered most: we were behind on two metrics and instead of pushing on blindly we pulled the social-media comparison video forward. That was the first time I saw "changing the plan" as Agile success rather than failure. Sprint 2 then showed the opposite lesson — a sprint that starts small (Pass page + feedback + mobile) closes cleanly, and the feedback that came out of it (mobile support) produced the most visible product change of the whole project.

05 · Evidence + Reflection

Agile Logbook

Daily stand-up, sprint plans, retrospective notes and the decisions that shaped the product.

Evidence — weekly cycles

Week 1 · 04–07 July 2026 — Business Model foundation + logo

Focus: Draft Business Model Canvas, explore logo directions, outline solution architecture, convert redesign HTML to semantic HTML.

Blockers: Logo concepts were too generic → decided not to force a final choice on day one. Architecture uncertainty → kept the build frontend-focused and documented future integration points.

Reflection: Starting with the business model first clarified who we served; that clarity later shaped the MVP messaging.

Week 2 · 14–17 July 2026 — MVP launch & campus activation

Planned: WhatsApp flyer, 4-question Google Form, 10 target groups, campus table.

Actual: 8/10 groups confirmed; 120+ flyers; 41 pre-registrations by 17 July.

Blockers: Two WhatsApp admins hadn't approved access → launched with 8 and added later. Trust scepticism → leading with the Golden Arrow partnership visibly improved openness.

Reflection: Gatekeeper approvals need lead time. Partner messaging outperformed feature messaging. Adjusted plan mid-cycle (brought social-media forward) instead of waiting passively.

Week 3 · 21–24 July 2026 — Trello + Figma wireframes

Planned: Board setup, 14 stories, MoSCoW, low-fi wireframes.

Actual: 6 Must-Have stories defined; 5 core screens wireframed; internal walkthrough caught navigation and trust issues on the same day.

Blockers: "Book a trip" too broad → split into three cards. Tracking screen felt sparse → added a status timeline.

Reflection: Same-day walkthrough caught problems while they were cheap to fix.

Week 4 · 28–31 July 2026 — Coded interface + sprint close

Planned: Polish Figma, code Home/Booking/Tracking, close sprint.

Actual: Home, Route selector, Booking and Tracking all coded; end-to-end flow tested; walkthrough video recorded; all cards in Done.

Blockers: Figma spacing vs. real mobile → mobile-first wins. Map integration out of scope → static map placeholder + "live tracking coming soon".

Reflection: Closing the sprint properly on Trello made hand-off to testing straightforward. Reusing status language from Figma saved time.

August 2026 — Architecture pivot, Supabase, live tracking

Focus: ERD implementation in four packages; hosting constraint forced a pivot from Spring Boot to Supabase.

Actual: Campus, User, Route, Stop, Bus, Driver, LiveTrip, Pass tables created. Registration → live tracking path working. TrackView.vue with Leaflet refreshes every 10 s.

Blockers: GitHub Pages can't run Java; UUID/bigint type mismatches; LiveTrip schema gaps; GPS unreliable in demos → dual GPS + manual input.

Reflection: Hosting constraints must be validated before locking backend tech. Iterative schema refinement is Agile behaviour, not failure.

September 2026 — Supabase integration, Pass page, mobile feedback

Focus: Connect backend to frontend; update tracking with map; Pass page; mobile support.

Actual: All main pages read/write live data. Dashboard gained live bus tracking. Pass page supports view/top-up/purchase. Feedback check-in revealed students mostly use phones → mobile support implemented before October.

Reflection: Testing with real users gave us the most important change of the month — mobile support — which we would never have found ourselves.

Reflection

Reading the logbook back, I can see my own growth in three phases. In July I was learning to finish things — closing a Trello sprint properly, not just leaving working files open. In August I learned to adapt — the Spring Boot to Supabase pivot would have derailed a rigid team, but because our logbook showed every decision and blocker, the team could move on without losing trust. In September I learned to listen — the feedback session produced mobile support, the most visible product change of the year, and it wasn't in anyone's original plan. The logbook is the artefact that proves this wasn't Agile theatre: every week has a plan, an actual, a blocker and a reflection, and every reflection is followed by a concrete change in the next sprint. That discipline — write it down, reflect, adjust — is the professional habit I am taking with me from PRP370S.
